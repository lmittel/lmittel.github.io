There are only 14 distinct calendars, one for each day of the week a year can start on, in common and leap years. From 1901 to 2099, where every fourth year is a leap year, they repeat in the same order every 28 years. In each cycle the common year calendars are used three times each and the leap year calendars once each, and every date falls on each day of the week exactly four times. The pattern breaks at century years, which are leap years only when divisible by 400, so 1900 and 2100 have no February 29 while 2000 does. The 15 years from 2000 to 2014 cover only part of a cycle and use 11 of the 14 calendars, so dates other than February 29 fell on a weekend unequal numbers of times (3, 4 or 5). Since fewer births occur on weekends, each extra weekend lowers a date's average by about 2%, so raw counts by date depend partly on the arbitrary start and end of the series rather than on the date itself. This is one reason a model is needed. It separates the effect of a date from the day of the week it happened to fall on.

The model

log births(t) =
  trend(t) + season(t) + week(t) + date(t) + ε(t)
  • Trend is a smooth curve with a time scale of about eight months.
  • Season is a smooth annual curve that drifts slowly over the period.
  • Week gives each day of the week its own level, which drifts over about two years.
  • Date gives each calendar date its own effect, separately for weekdays and weekends, with small effects shrunk toward zero. Memorial Day, Labor Day, Thanksgiving and the days around them get their own effects, as do weekdays taken off around holidays near a weekend.
